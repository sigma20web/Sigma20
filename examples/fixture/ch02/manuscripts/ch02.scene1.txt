5:52 a.m. Gate 3, Mapo.

Do-yoon had been standing at the barrier for eleven minutes.

The gate hung two meters above the loading yard, a hole in the air the color of a bruise, and the cold coming off it was the wrong kind of cold — dry, static, like the inside of a server room. Six months from now every porter in the city would know that smell. This morning there were four people in the yard and none of them were looking at it.

Park Mu-jin arrived at 5:58 with a paper cup in each hand.

“You're early.” He said it like an accusation.

“You said six.”

“I say six to everybody. Nobody comes at six.” Mu-jin pushed one of the cups at him. It was terrible coffee. Do-yoon drank it. “You're the F-rank from the board. Kang.”

“Kang Do-yoon.”

“Mm.” Mu-jin looked him over the way a man looks over a secondhand ladder. “Eight percent. You carry what I tell you, you walk where I walk, and when I say run you are already running. Say yes.”

“Yes.”

“Good.” He drained his own cup, crushed it, and pocketed the crushed cup instead of dropping it, which was the first thing Do-yoon had ever liked about him. “D-grade. Spiders, mostly. Two chambers, one corridor between them. Forty minutes in, forty out, and if you throw up you do it outside the ring.”

*Three chambers.*

Do-yoon did not say it. The Association's survey had been filed nine days ago by a man who had counted two chambers because he had not walked to the end of the corridor. In four hours that clerical decision would put Mu-jin on a stretcher.

“Mister Park,” he said. “Which corridor do you take?”

“Left. Always left. Shorter.”

“Is it shorter?”

Mu-jin stopped with his hand on the barrier gate.

“It's shorter,” he said, slowly, “because the right one bends around the water.”

“Then it isn't shorter. It's drier.”

Behind them the yard lights cut out — 6:00 exactly, on a municipal timer that did not care what was standing in the air above the loading dock. In the sudden grey the gate was the brightest thing in Mapo.

“Get your bag,” Mu-jin said.
