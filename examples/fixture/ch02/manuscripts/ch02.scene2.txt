The first chamber took nineteen minutes and cost them nothing.

Do-yoon carried sixty kilos of rope, spare cores and water, three steps behind Mu-jin's right heel — where a porter was supposed to walk, and also where he could see the older man's whole back. Two hunters worked the front. The spiders came off the ceiling in ones and twos and died in ones and twos.

At the mouth of the corridor Mu-jin turned left.

“Mister Park.”

“What.”

“The right one.”

“The right one is eleven minutes longer.”

“Yes,” Do-yoon said.

Mu-jin turned around fully, which he had not done once inside the gate.

“Give me a reason. Not a feeling. A reason.”

Do-yoon had prepared four. Three of them were lies, and the lies were better, and he heard himself use the fourth.

“The survey counted two chambers. There are three. The third sits above the left corridor and it has been holding water for nine days.” He shifted the bag. “If anything comes down through that ceiling, it comes down on the left. On the right we'd have the bend between us and it.”

The nearer hunter laughed once, at the ceiling.

Mu-jin did not laugh. He looked down the left corridor for four full seconds, and Do-yoon watched a man do arithmetic he had no data for and arrive, by whatever route veterans arrive, at the answer.

“Right corridor.”

“Boss—”

“Right corridor. Eleven minutes won't kill anybody.” He was already walking. “Kang. If you're wrong, you carry for free.”

They were nine meters in when the sound started behind them: the wet, patient grinding of a ceiling that had been holding a chamber's worth of water for nine days and had finished thinking about it.

Nobody ran. It was Mu-jin who got a fist into the nearer hunter's collar and hauled him backward around the bend.

“Water,” he said. “You can't cut water.”

The left corridor emptied itself into the first chamber for a long time. Do-yoon counted it against his own pulse — eleven seconds of falling masonry, then the slower, uglier sound of a room filling. Grit came around the bend in a warm gust and settled on all four of them.

“That was where we were going,” the nearer hunter said, to nobody.

“That was where we were going,” Mu-jin agreed. He did not look at Do-yoon. He took out a cigarette he was not allowed to light inside a gate, held it, and put it back. “Kang. You carry for eighteen percent.”

“Mister Park—”

“Where did you get the third chamber?”

“A porter told me. One who isn't working anymore.”

Mu-jin looked at him for a long moment, then nodded once at an answer he had decided not to open.

They walked out of Mapo Gate 3 at 7:06 with four people, eleven cores, and an old man's left leg still attached to the rest of him.

The stones went into the counting window at 7:40. The reader chirped. Then it chirped differently.

“These are B-grade,” the clerk said.

“It's a D-gate,” said the hunter who had laughed at the ceiling.

“I know what it is.” She turned one of the cores over as if the underside would argue with her. “Eleven cores, nine of them two grades above envelope. I have to flag the gate.”

Do-yoon kept his hands on the bag strap.

Mapo Gate 3 had been a D-gate the first time too. He had carried its cores to this window, in this line, and been paid eight percent of nothing much.

Nothing about this morning should have changed the stones.

Unless the third chamber had never been holding water at all.
